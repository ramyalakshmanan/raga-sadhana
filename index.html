The building blocks

The twelve swarasthanas, how all 72 melakartas are built, and the talas that carry the music.

The twelve swarasthanas

Swarasthanam (swara + sthanam, “place”) is the exact pitch a swara sits at, measured from Sa. Sa and Pa never move. Ri, Ga, Ma, Dha and Ni each have more than one sthanam, and together they fill twelve positions in the octave. Singing a raga in tune means landing each swara on its own sthanam. Tap a position to hear it.

Decode any melakarta

A melakarta is a full scale with all seven swaras, going straight up and straight down. Sa and Pa never change. The only differences are which Ri and Ga, which Ma, and which Dha and Ni it uses.

Janya vocabulary

Janya
A raga derived from a melakarta. It may skip swaras or move through them in a zigzag.
Varjya
Omitted swaras. Mohanam omits Ma and Ni.
Audava · Shadava · Sampurna
Five, six or seven swaras in one direction. Mohanam is audava–audava; Bilahari is audava–sampurna.
Vakra
A zigzag order, like Kedaram's S M G M P N Ṡ.
Upanga · Bhashanga
An upanga raga stays within its parent's swaras. A bhashanga raga borrows an outside swara (anya swara), as Bhairavi and Kambhoji do. The library is mostly upanga ragas; Kambhoji is included and marked bhashanga, with its anya swara shown.

What is a tala?

A tala is the rhythmic cycle a piece is set in. One full cycle is an avartanam, each beat in it is an akshara, and the first beat, where the cycle starts again, is samam. Singers keep tala with the hand, using claps, finger counts and waves called kriyas, so the cycle can be seen as well as felt. Every tala is built from three parts, the angas:

Talas in everyday use

Most of what you will sing is in a handful of talas. Choose one to load it into the player.

The 35 talas

There are seven suladi talas, and each can be set in five jatis. The jati sets the length of the laghu; drutam and anudrutam never change. The number in each cell is the count of aksharas in one avartanam. Highlighted cells are the forms in everyday use. Choose any cell to play it.

Gati (nadai)

The tala sets how many beats make a cycle; the gati sets how many pulses fill each beat. Chatusra gati, four to a beat, is the usual one. Tisra gati, three to a beat, has a lilting feel, while khanda (five) and misra (seven) appear in rhythmic passages and percussion. Seven talas in five jatis, each in five gatis, give the 175 talas of the system.

Chapu talas

Chapu talas come from folk and dance traditions and are counted in uneven groups rather than with laghu and drutam. Misra chapu (3 + 2 + 2, seven beats) and khanda chapu (2 + 3, five beats) are the most common, and many kritis are set in misra chapu.

Choose a raga above, press Start and sing. When you stop (or after 4 seconds of silence), you get a report on every swara you sang: which swara the app heard, how many cents sharp or flat you were, and how steadily you held it. Start the tambura first so you have Sa to sing against.

Turn the tambura off for this one. Sing your lowest comfortable note, with no strain or growl, and hold it while the app listens for five seconds. Then do the same for your highest comfortable note. Carnatic singing usually spans about two octaves, from mandra Pa (P̣) to tara Pa (Ṗ).

Sustain one swara against the shruti and see how steady you are. Start the tambura for a reference. Singing Sa and Pa in tune is the foundation of every other exercise.

Climb the raga one swara at a time. Hold each target until it turns green; the app moves on by itself. It measures how long you take to find each swara and whether you sit sharp or flat on it.

Leap between octaves and land in tune: Sa to tara Sa, mandra Pa to Pa, and each raga swara to its octave. The app times how fast you settle on the new note. Jumps stay inside your recorded range, or mandra Pa to tara Pa if you have not recorded one.

Sing freely in the chosen raga: an alapana, a varnam line, a kriti. The app counts how much of your voiced time sits on the raga's swaras and which outside swaras you slip into. Gamakas pass through other pitches on purpose, so treat this as a guide, not a grade.
